Case study · 2026 · Production — my own business

Running a multi-channel publishing operation with scheduled agents

Ten scheduled agent workflows that research, draft, illustrate and publish content across five channels for Sukses Dengan Emas, my own gold-savings brand. It runs without me watching it, it writes into systems that are not mine, and it has a human approval gate before anything goes public.

The problem

My brand publishes daily: an article on the website, an Instagram and Facebook post in the morning and again in the afternoon, a LinkedIn piece, and a TikTok caption on weekdays. Done by hand that is most of a working day, every day, and it stops the moment I get busy — which is exactly when a content business cannot afford to stop. The specific problem was not "I want AI to write for me". It was: how do I keep a five-channel publishing schedule running at a consistent standard without spending my day on it, and without publishing anything I have not approved?

The day five runs died — and what I did about it

On 15 October 2026 five scheduled runs failed between 00:00 and 02:25. Each logged the same error: the model provider had stopped responding, and the runtime aborted the call after five to seven unanswered attempts. No articles, no posts — the publishing schedule that day produced nothing.

  1. Read the logs before guessing. The five failures shared one signature, so the content was not the problem and the schedules were not the problem.
  2. Tested the provider directly, outside the workflow. A minimal API call proved the provider was answering some requests and abandoning others — intermittent congestion, not a broken key or a bad request. A second model on the same provider answered every probe.
  3. Split the fix three ways. Pin the scheduled workflows to the model that answers reliably (the interactive sessions keep the faster one, which is fine because a human notices a stall and a cron job does not). Bound every job's work — a maximum tool-call budget, a two-strike rule for unreachable sources, and an instruction to deliver partial results rather than retry forever. And add a backup run later in the day that checks whether the day's output already exists and only works if it does not.

This is the part of agent operations I would want to talk about in an interview: unattended systems fail most often at the boundary you do not own — the model provider, the third-party API, the network. Designing for that boundary is a different skill from writing the prompt.

Limitations — stated, not buried

  • Single user, own business. No multi-tenant design, no team, no service level agreement. I am the only operator and the only approver.
  • I have not measured the time saved. I can count runs and published articles. I cannot honestly convert that into hours saved, because I never logged the manual baseline.
  • Human approval is a real gate, and a bottleneck. Nothing publishes without my review, which means throughput is capped by my attention. Review time is not logged, so I cannot quote it.
  • Failures at the provider boundary are expected, not exceptional. The mitigation reduces the blast radius; it does not remove the dependency.
  • Model capability is the ceiling on quality. The agents follow procedure well and still produce work I edit. I would not represent its output as publication-ready without review.
  • No machine learning. Nothing here trains or fine-tunes a model. The skill is agent configuration, orchestration, verification and incident handling.
