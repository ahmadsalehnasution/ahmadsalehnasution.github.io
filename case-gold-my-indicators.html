Gold vs Malaysian household indicators

An eight-query SQL model in DuckDB that joins the gold price in ringgit against Malaysian consumer prices, retail trade, household income and unemployment — to test the claim that gold protects a household's purchasing power, and to show where that claim stops holding.

Public data only · every published figure produced by a query in this repository · cross-source validation before publication

Open the dashboard →

The problem

Gold is sold in Malaysia as an inflation hedge, and the pitch is usually just "the price keeps going up". That claim is only testable against a household's own numbers: how fast are consumer prices actually rising, what does a gram cost relative to a median monthly income, and how much of the rise is the gold price rather than the ringgit. A retailer who can answer those three questions sells — and prices — differently.

What I did, and what the agent did

  • I set the question and chose the sources. Malaysian public data from data.gov.my (OpenDOSM), daily gold futures prices and European Central Bank reference rates. Sources that blocked automated access were dropped rather than scraped by hand.
  • The agent built the pipeline. Fetch, load into DuckDB, write the query framework, run everything, render the result tables, and flag data-quality problems such as partial months.
  • I wrote the validation check and read the results critically. A correlation of 0.944 between the gold price and retail sales appears in query 07 — it is an artefact of two upward trends and demonstrates nothing causal. Catching that is my job, not the agent's.

The most useful output is not the headline return. It is that the same series everyone calls a safe haven gave back an eighth of its value in the eight months after its peak — and that affordability is 2.6× harder for a median household in Kelantan than in Kuala Lumpur, for an identical product.

Limitations

  • The gold series is COMEX futures, not the Malaysian retail price. Dealer premiums and buy/sell spreads are not included, so this is the international price converted to ringgit — not what a walk-in customer pays.
  • Part of the ringgit gain is ringgit weakness, not a stronger gold market. A USD-denominated version of the same queries is the next step and is not done yet.
  • Household income comes from a two-yearly survey (latest 2024) joined onto a monthly price series, so the affordability figures are indicative and 2025–2026 affordability is estimated on a 2024 income baseline.
  • A correlation of 0.944 between gold and retail sales is not evidence of anything: both series trend upward. Differenced or seasonally adjusted series would be needed for a real test.
  • No transaction costs, taxes or zakat are modelled, so the real return shown is a price return only.
  • The current month is partial at fetch time (9 trading days) and is flagged in the data-quality column rather than compared against full months.
  • No commercial outcome is measured. No revenue or conversion claim is made.
